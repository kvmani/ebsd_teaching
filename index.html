Common misconception

Kikuchi bands are not caused by surface roughness or contamination. They arise from coherent diffraction within the crystal according to Bragg’s law.

What students should notice

Indexing works when enough sharp bands are visible with good signal and without saturation. Improve signal or detail, or reduce speed/drift.

Try this comparison

Switch between Fast survey and High quality. Fast scans update quickly but show noisier colors; high quality scans sharpen bands at the cost of time.

Noisy indexing

Possible cause: low exposure/current. Observe: unstable colors and weak confidence-like cues. First checks: add exposure, beam current, binning, or averaging.

Gain clipping

Cause: too much gain/current. Observe: bright saturated pixels. Fix: lower gain, then recover signal with exposure if needed.

Coarse pixels / drift

Cause: high binning or unstable stage. Observe: blocky or warped boundaries. Fix: reduce binning and stabilize before scanning.

Conceptual explanation

How Kikuchi Bands Are Indexed

This module is a simplified indexing workflow, not a full commercial indexing engine. It shows why indexing depends on pattern quality, calibration, phase selection, band geometry, and confidence metrics.

This studio explains EBSD indexing logic using schematic overlays and simplified scoring. It is not a real Hough transform, crystallographic solver, or calibrated indexing engine.

1. Raw Kikuchi pattern

The detector records a diffraction pattern with bands, background intensity, noise, and possible saturation.

What can go wrong: weak bands, damaged surfaces, poor exposure, drift, or clipping can hide useful features.

Checkpoint: Can a bright raw pattern still be hard to index?

2. Background correction

Smooth background is reduced so Kikuchi bands stand out more clearly from diffuse intensity.

What can go wrong: over-correction can distort bands; under-correction leaves bands hard to detect.

Checkpoint: Why remove smooth background?

3. Band detection

Real EBSD software can search for band-like line features; this app only illustrates conceptual positions, widths, and angles.

What can go wrong: low thresholds find false bands; high thresholds miss weak real bands.

Checkpoint: What is being detected?

4. Hough transform

A Hough-style transform can help detect Kikuchi bands as lines or line-like features by mapping image features into a voting space.

What can go wrong: noise, overlap, and bad background correction can create ambiguous peaks.

Checkpoint: What does the Hough transform help find?

5. Band angle matching

Conceptual band positions and angles are compared with theoretical crystal plane geometry for the selected phase.

What can go wrong: wrong phase choice, pseudosymmetry, or bad detector calibration can produce plausible but wrong matches.

Checkpoint: What is compared during matching?

6. Orientation solution

The schematic best match illustrates a candidate crystal orientation, often expressed in real workflows through Euler angles, matrices, or IPF map colors.

What can go wrong: a solution can look tidy but still be wrong if the setup or phase model is wrong.

Checkpoint: Does a good-looking map prove the solution is correct?

7. Confidence and failure modes

Indexing is not magic. Confidence-like metrics help judge decision strength, but they are not proof of physical truth. Pattern quality, calibration, phase selection, and thresholds all matter.

What can go wrong: good-looking EBSD maps can still be wrong, especially with wrong phase lists, damaged patterns, pseudosymmetry, or poor calibration.

Checkpoint: Which evidence should you inspect before trusting a map?
